Features

Built around how the work actually gets recorded

Not a generic tracker bolted onto a spreadsheet — every part of the flow, from the weekly grid to the audit trail, assumes hours get reviewed by someone.

Weekly timesheets that hold

Pick a week, fill the grid per project and task, save as a draft. Copy last week forward when the work repeats. Daily and weekly caps are enforced on the server, not just in the form.

Approvals without the back-and-forth

Leads approve or request changes inline, project by project — so one disputed line doesn't hold up an otherwise clean week.

Edit requests, not silent edits

Once a week is submitted it locks. Changing a submitted entry means raising a request that a reviewer sees and signs off — the correction is part of the record.

Dashboards that answer real questions

Hours by project, by task and by member, filtered to any range. Estimates sit next to actuals, and deep search finds a single task's hours across the whole history.

Five roles, enforced server-side

Admin, CEO, PM, Team Lead and Team Member each see a different slice. Scoping is applied at the API — hiding a button was never the security model.

An append-only audit trail

Every state change is written with its before and after values, attributed to whoever made it. Log rows are only ever inserted — never updated, never deleted.

How it works

Four steps, start to signed off

1

Log your hours

Fill the week's grid against the projects and tasks you're assigned to. Drafts save as you go.

2

Submit for review

Submitting locks the week, so the numbers under review can't shift while someone's reading them.

3

Your lead responds

Approved, or sent back with a note saying what to fix. Either way it lands in your inbox.

4

It rolls into reporting

Approved hours flow straight into the dashboards. Every figure you see there has been signed off.

What this
actually is

Time Ops is an internal tool for teams who run client projects and need the hours to be defensible — not just collected. It's built for organisations where the person logging time, the person reviewing it and the person reporting on it are three different people with three different levels of access.

The problem it solves is the gap between the spreadsheet everyone actually fills in and the number that ends up in a report. In between sit the corrections, the approvals and the "who changed this?" — the parts a spreadsheet quietly loses. Here they're first-class: submission locks the week, corrections go through a reviewer, and every change keeps its before-and-after.

It is not a billing system and it does not price your work. It tracks hours, who approved them, and what they were spent on.
